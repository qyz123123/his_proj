## Cisco: Networking Hardware and the Public Experience of Being Connected

Cisco was founded in 1984 by Leonard Bosack and Sandy Lerner, who were associated with Stanford University. The company's early purpose was practical and technical: to connect different computer networks. At the time, many networks existed as separate systems, often using different protocols and equipment. Cisco's routers helped data move between networks, making it possible for institutions to communicate more effectively. This may sound distant from ordinary computer or smartphone users, but it is central to the history of adoption. A computer becomes far more valuable when it is connected to other computers.

During the late 1980s and 1990s, Cisco became one of the most important suppliers of routers and switches, the equipment that directs traffic across networks. As the internet expanded from academic and government environments into business and public life, Cisco benefited enormously. Its equipment helped build the infrastructure that carried email, web pages, enterprise data, and later streaming media and cloud services. The public usually experienced the internet through browsers and websites, but companies like Cisco made that experience physically possible.

Cisco's significance is best understood through the changing meaning of a computer. In the early personal computer era, a PC could be useful as a standalone device for writing, calculating, gaming, or printing. But networked computing changed everything. Once connected, a computer became a communication device, a research tool, a shopping terminal, a workplace portal, and a social space. Cisco helped create the conditions for this shift by improving the reliability and scale of networking.

The 1990s internet boom made networking infrastructure a public concern even when users did not know the names of the devices involved. Businesses wanted websites, universities wanted connected campuses, governments wanted digital services, and homes eventually wanted broadband. Every new use case increased the value of owning a computer. A family might buy a PC not only for homework but also for email, news, travel planning, and online shopping. Cisco's routers and switches were part of the hidden system that made those experiences possible.

Cisco also shaped enterprise adoption. Companies built internal networks, connected branch offices, and later adopted internet-based communication systems. Workers learned to use networked computers as part of daily routines. This workplace familiarity helped spread digital skills into the general population. People often adopt technologies at home after learning them at school or work. Cisco's role in enterprise networking therefore contributed indirectly to public comfort with digital devices.

In the smartphone era, Cisco's contribution remained important even though the company did not dominate consumer handsets. Smartphones depend on networks. Mobile apps, cloud storage, messaging, video calls, maps, and streaming services require reliable connectivity between devices, data centers, and service providers. Cisco's networking technologies and security products helped support the broader internet infrastructure on which smartphone experiences depend. The public adopted smartphones partly because they were not isolated gadgets. They were always-connected portals.

This makes Cisco a useful corrective to device-centered histories. The public may remember the phone brand, the computer brand, or the app, but adoption also required massive investment in routers, switches, network management, and security. Without that infrastructure, digital devices would have been far less persuasive.

For the DQ, Cisco shows that U.S. technology companies contributed to adoption by making connection normal. The general public did not adopt computers and smartphones only to possess hardware. They adopted them to reach people, information, services, and communities. Cisco's history reminds us that the adoption of digital devices depends on networks that users rarely see. Its contribution was to help transform computers from individual tools into nodes in a global communication system. That transformation made computers and smartphones much more compelling to ordinary people.
