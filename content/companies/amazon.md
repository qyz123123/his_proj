## Amazon: E-Commerce, Cloud Computing, and the Everyday Usefulness of Devices

Amazon was founded in 1994 by Jeff Bezos and began as an online bookstore. That beginning was modest compared with the company's later scale, but it was historically important. Books were searchable, catalog-friendly, and easy to ship, which made them a strong early product for demonstrating internet commerce. Amazon showed ordinary users that a computer connected to the internet could do something practical: find a product, compare information, place an order, and receive a delivery. This helped make the internet feel useful in daily life.

In the late 1990s, many people were still unsure what the internet was for. Email and web browsing were important, but Amazon gave the public a commercial reason to go online. Its website combined search, recommendations, customer reviews, shopping carts, and secure checkout. Each feature taught users a new digital habit. Searching for products taught confidence in online information. Reviews taught users to trust other users. Recommendations taught them that software could personalize choices. Checkout taught them to trust digital payments. These habits later transferred easily to smartphone apps.

Amazon expanded beyond books into a vast marketplace. This growth changed public expectations about convenience. Consumers became accustomed to broad selection, quick comparison, and home delivery. The company also invested heavily in logistics, warehouses, and fulfillment systems. This physical infrastructure is important because digital adoption often depends on whether online actions produce reliable offline results. When an order placed on a computer or phone arrives correctly, users gain confidence in digital systems.

Amazon Prime, introduced in 2005, strengthened this relationship by connecting membership, fast shipping, streaming media, and customer loyalty. Prime made Amazon not just a website but a routine. That routine increased the value of computers and smartphones because shopping, entertainment, and account management became available through screens. A smartphone with the Amazon app became a store in a pocket, a price checker, a media device, and a household management tool.

Amazon's second major contribution was Amazon Web Services, launched in 2006. AWS allowed companies and developers to rent computing power, storage, databases, and other infrastructure instead of building everything themselves. This lowered the cost of creating websites and apps. Many services that people use on computers and smartphones depend on cloud infrastructure like AWS. In this sense, Amazon helped expand the app economy by making it easier for new digital products to launch and scale.

The company's devices and services, including Kindle, Echo, and Alexa, also shaped public relationships with digital technology. Kindle made digital reading more acceptable by focusing on convenience and access. Echo and Alexa introduced voice interaction into homes, showing another way computers could become ambient rather than desk-bound. These products were not all equally transformative, but they reflected Amazon's larger strategy: embed computing into ordinary routines.

This is why Amazon's history is especially interesting for studying adoption. It did not persuade the public through a single device. It persuaded users by repeatedly connecting digital action to visible reward: a package arrived, a book downloaded, a movie streamed, an app stayed online. Those repeated confirmations made computers and smartphones feel trustworthy.

For the DQ, Amazon shows how U.S. technology companies contributed to adoption by making computers and smartphones useful for daily consumption and services. People adopt devices when those devices solve repeated problems: buying goods, reading reviews, watching media, managing subscriptions, and accessing cloud-supported apps. Amazon connected the digital screen to real-world convenience. It also built cloud infrastructure that allowed countless other companies to offer services through computers and phones. Its history makes clear that adoption is driven not only by devices themselves but by the useful activities that companies build around them.
