## Meta: Social Networks and the Emotional Reason to Carry a Smartphone

Meta began as Facebook in 2004, founded by Mark Zuckerberg and fellow students at Harvard. It started as a campus social network, but its rapid expansion showed that the internet was not only a library, store, or workplace. It could also be a social environment. This was a major shift in the adoption of computers and smartphones. People may buy devices for practical reasons, but they use them constantly when those devices connect them to other people.

Facebook's early appeal came from identity and relationship. Users created profiles, listed interests, connected with friends, posted updates, shared photographs, and joined groups. These actions seem ordinary today, but they represented a new way of making social life visible and searchable. Computers became tools for maintaining relationships, remembering events, and performing identity. That emotional and social value helped move digital technology beyond work and research.

As Facebook opened beyond universities, it became a global social platform. The News Feed, introduced in 2006, changed the experience of the site by turning scattered profile updates into a continuous stream. This design encouraged repeated checking and made social information feel immediate. The public learned to treat the computer, and later the smartphone, as a live window into friends, news, entertainment, and public conversation. This was not a neutral change. It raised questions about attention, privacy, misinformation, and platform power. But as a force for adoption, it was extremely effective.

Meta's acquisitions of Instagram in 2012 and WhatsApp in 2014 deepened its role in the smartphone era. Instagram connected mobile photography, filters, visual identity, and social feedback. It made the smartphone camera socially central. Users did not simply take pictures; they shared moments, built audiences, followed celebrities, discovered products, and participated in visual culture. WhatsApp, meanwhile, became essential messaging infrastructure in many countries. It showed that smartphones could replace or reshape older communication systems through internet-based messaging.

The smartphone became the perfect device for social platforms because it was personal, portable, camera-equipped, and always connected. Meta's services gave users powerful reasons to carry and check their phones throughout the day. Notifications, messages, comments, likes, stories, reels, and group chats made the phone feel socially necessary. This is one of the clearest examples of how software drives hardware adoption. People adopted smartphones not only because the devices were technically impressive, but because social platforms made absence feel costly.

Facebook's 2021 rebranding as Meta signaled an ambition to move beyond social networking into virtual reality, augmented reality, and immersive digital environments. Whether that strategy succeeds fully is still debated, but it continues the company's long interest in mediating social presence through technology. The company's investments in VR also show that adoption depends on whether new devices can create compelling social uses, not merely technical novelty.

This makes Meta's history both fascinating and uncomfortable. It reveals that adoption is not always driven by rational efficiency. It is also driven by belonging, curiosity, popularity, and fear of missing out. Those forces made the smartphone extraordinarily sticky.

For the DQ, Meta shows how U.S. technology companies contributed to adoption by creating social demand. Computers and smartphones became general-public technologies because they connected people to relationships, communities, images, messages, and public conversation. Meta's history also requires critical commentary. The same designs that made smartphones useful and engaging also raised concerns about addiction, privacy, political polarization, and mental health. Still, those concerns prove the depth of adoption. Meta helped make digital devices emotionally central to daily life. It turned the smartphone into a social instrument that many people felt they could not ignore.
