### Moore’s Law: The Expectation That Made Better PCs Seem Inevitable

By *Yunzhe Qu*

Moore’s Law is not a laptop, a chip model, or an operating system.

It is an idea.

But in the history of personal computing, this idea behaved almost like a product promise: computers would keep getting smaller, faster, and cheaper.

Gordon Moore described the rapid increase of components on integrated circuits in 1965. Over time, people called this pattern **Moore’s Law**.

Why does that matter?

Because adoption depends on belief.

Ordinary buyers, schools, and businesses needed to believe that personal computers were not temporary toys. They needed to believe that the machines would improve and become more useful. Moore’s Law helped create that confidence.

BUT, **MOORE’S LAW DID MORE THAN PREDICT PROGRESS**

It pushed the industry to deliver progress. Chip companies, computer makers, and software developers planned around the expectation that future machines would have more power. Better chips made better software possible. Better software gave people more reasons to buy computers.

This cycle helped personal computers spread.

A PC that once handled simple text could later handle graphics, games, internet browsers, music, video, and complex school or office work. **The machine kept becoming more worth owning.**

Moore’s Law also helped lower costs. As technology improved, more computing power could reach ordinary consumers.

For the driving question, Moore’s Law shows that personal computer adoption was not one single event.

It was a long wave of improvement.

Moore’s Law made that wave feel believable.
